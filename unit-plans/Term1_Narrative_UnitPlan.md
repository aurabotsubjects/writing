## PART A — ACS UNIT PLAN FRONT MATTER

### UNDERSTAND

**Universal Concept:** *Reflections on Life — The Things That Are Important to Us*
Identity and belonging · Values and beliefs · Connections and relationships · Resilience and challenges · Joy and gratitude.

**Big Idea (Writing):**
Writers turn small, real moments of their own lives into stories that let a reader feel what they felt. A story worth telling is not the biggest event — it is the moment that *mattered*.

**Throughline Question:**
*"Why do experiences, relationships, and values shape who we are, and how do they influence what truly matters in our lives?"*

**Writing Throughline (student-facing, used all term):**
**"What moment made me who I am — and how do I make you feel it too?"**

**Biblical Perspective**

| Element | This term |
|---|---|
| Framework | **Creation → Fall → Redemption → Restoration** — every good narrative arc mirrors this shape: something good, something broken, something rescued, something made new. Students are taught the story arc *through* this lens. |
| Attributes of God | God as **Storyteller** — He tells His story through the lives of real people (Ruth, David, the disciples). Our stories sit inside His. |
| Principle | Developing faith and character · Learning about people and how to think |
| Motto | **Soli Deo Gloria** — we craft our words well because the gift of language came from Him. |

**Anchor Value: AROHA — Love: God, Others, Self, World.**
Narrative prompts, jotter entries and the celebration all centre on relationship, kindness, connection, and the moments where love was present (or missing).

**Key Scriptures**

- *Psalm 139:13–16* — "You knit me together" (Week 1 launch: your story began before you did).
- *Genesis 1:27* — made in His image, therefore makers ourselves (Week 1).
- *Deuteronomy 6:6–9* — telling our stories to the next generation (Week 3: audience).
- *Colossians 3:23* — "Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart" (Weeks 8–9: recrafting is an act of care).
- *Proverbs 25:11* — "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver" (Week 7: word choice).

**Mātauranga Māori / Te Tiriti connection:**
**Tūrangawaewae** — a place to stand. Narrative writing this term is anchored in place: the beach, the marae, the back paddock, Nana's kitchen, the court at lunchtime. Students identify *where* their moment happened and what that place means. Links to **whakapapa** (our stories are inherited) and **kōrero tuku iho** (stories handed down) — Week 2 includes a "story from my whānau" jotter task.

### KNOW — Curriculum Links

**NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — Writing**

*Composition — Audience, purpose and task*
- identify the audience and purpose for a text
- plan and write with the audience in mind, selecting the appropriate genre, language register, and word choice to best communicate the intended meaning
- understand that different audiences may have a very different response to the same text

*Sentence structures and punctuation*
- demonstrate understanding of incomplete (fragment) and run-on sentences
- combine sentences orally, inserting phrases and varying the position of the phrase
- use a range of sentence structures and types to suit purpose and enhance impact
- expand sentences using adverbial phrases
- use beginning and ending sentence punctuation correctly and consistently across multi-paragraph texts
- use commas correctly for certain complex sentences, phrases and transition words, with some support
- **use speech marks and commas correctly for direct speech, with some support**

*Writing to entertain*
- draw on their own experiences and knowledge to write texts that entertain
- group ideas into paragraphs; use linking words and phrases; use topic-related vocabulary

*Writing craft — word choice & language features*
- select and use words and phrases that clearly express where and when things happen
- **use figurative language such as simile and metaphor to purposefully create vivid images and effects to engage the reader**

*Writing processes*
- Planning: use graphic organisers to organise and plan; make notes reducing information to key words; set writing goals from reflection
- Drafting: transfer ideas from organisers into sentences; paragraph layout conventions
- Revising: reread at sentence, paragraph and whole-text level; make revisions to improve clarity for the audience (replacing words, adding sentences, using peer feedback)
- Editing: edit using known punctuation; use a word card or dictionary; **give and receive peer feedback**
- Handwriting: handwrite with increasing stamina and fluency, maintaining legibility across multi-paragraph texts

**NZC English, Phase 2 — Oral Language (used in oral rehearsal, Helping Circle, sharing)**
- give descriptions, recounts and narrative retellings with specific details to actively engage listeners
- make comments that encourage discussion and ask clarifying questions
- communicate in complete sentences with a range of conjunctions and connectives, varying structure and length for effect

**NZC English, Phase 2 — Reading (mentor text work)**
- identify the audience and purpose of texts that entertain
- determine the structure of texts and how ideas are conveyed through text features
- respond to texts by sharing opinions and personal thoughts and feelings about ideas in texts

**Knowledge outcomes — students will know that…**
1. A narrative has a shape: orientation → complication → rising tension → climax → resolution → reflection.
2. Small-moment stories (one event, one place, minutes long) are usually stronger than "my whole holiday" stories.
3. Showing (action, senses, dialogue, body language) creates more impact than telling (naming the emotion).
4. Dialogue is punctuated with speech marks, a comma before the closing mark, and a new line for each new speaker.
5. Sentence length is a tool: short sentences speed a reader up at the tense part; longer sentences slow them down.
6. Writers write for someone. The audience changes word choice, detail and tone.
7. Real writers revise. A first draft is thinking; a second draft is craft.

**Possible misconceptions**
- ⚠ "A good story has to have lots of things happening." (More events ≠ better; depth beats breadth.)
- ⚠ "Revising means fixing spelling." (Revising = add, cut, reorder, replace ideas. Editing = surface features. Both, in that order.)
- ⚠ "Descriptive writing means more adjectives." (Precision beats quantity — one exact verb beats three adjectives.)
- ⚠ "It has to be true." (Personal narrative starts in truth but a writer may reshape for impact — we teach honest reshaping, not invention of events they'll present as fact.)
- ⚠ "Once it's finished, it's finished." (Publication is a decision, not an accident.)

**Essential Questions (used as WALTs)**
1. What makes a moment worth writing about?
2. How do writers make a reader *feel* something instead of just telling them?
3. Who am I writing for, and how does that change how I write?
4. How do I know if my writing is working?
5. What does it look like to make my writing better rather than just neater?

## PART C — THE WEEKLY RHYTHM

Every week has the same 5-day shape, so students learn the pattern, not just the content:

| Day | Role | Balance |
|---|---|---|
| **Monday** | **Launch** — new focus introduced through a model or teacher demonstration | I Do–heavy |
| **Tuesday** | **Build together** — the class makes an example jointly | We Do–heavy |
| **Wednesday** | **Stretch** — trickier application of the same skill | We Do → You Do bridge |
| **Thursday** | **Write** — students apply the focus to their own piece | You Do–heavy (teacher circulates + pulls a small group) |
| **Friday** | **Share & reflect** — Author's Chair, partner share, or Helping Circle | Student voice, minimal teacher input |

**Every 25-minute lesson:**

1. **Warm-Up Bridge (2 min)** — links today to that week's Daily Warm-Up skill.
2. **Process Check (1 min)** — hands, name the stage, one student says how they know. See Part D2.
3. **I Do (5–8 min)** — teacher models and thinks aloud (instruction → example → think-aloud → tip).
4. **We Do (5–8 min)** — joint construction; teacher scribes, class supplies (shared sentences + a discussion prompt).
5. **You Do (7–10 min)** — independent or paired writing, as a numbered task list.
6. **Close (1–2 min)** — one share, one preview of tomorrow.

Every lesson in the app is written out in full in this format — WALT, NZC link, curriculum tags, process check, and scripted I Do / We Do / You Do — matching the Daily Warm-Up's lesson-plan structure so the two apps feel like one programme.

**A note on the Warm-Up Bridge:** the Daily Warm-Up app runs Monday–Thursday. Friday's Writing Focus lesson therefore bridges back to *the week's* warm-up learning as a whole rather than to a single day.

## PART D — THE IDEA-SUPPORT SYSTEM (identical every week)

For students who freeze at a blank page. Each week has one prompt with four layers underneath; students take as many layers as they need.

- **Layer 1 — The Prompt.** One open sentence or scenario.
- **Layer 2 — Question Ladder.** 4 questions that walk a student from nothing to a first sentence.
- **Layer 3 — Word & Starter Bank.** 8–10 Tier 2 words + 3 sentence starters, drawn from that week's warm-up where possible.
- **Layer 4 — "Still Stuck?" (click to reveal).** A borrow-this-idea scenario · an image prompt · a "tell it to a partner first" instruction.

In the app, Layer 4 stays hidden behind a button so it never feels like a crutch to the kids who don't need it.

**Printable student worksheet.** Every week's prompt can be printed as a two-page student planning sheet (**📝 Print student worksheet** in the app): the prompt, the question ladder with writing space, the word bank as tick-boxes, the sentence starters, a plan/notes page, a box for the first sentence, the "only if you're stuck" layer, and a four-point self-check. It sits beside the student while they start writing — one sheet per week, and it doubles as evidence of the planning stage.

## PART D2 — TEACHING OUR WRITING PROCESS EXPLICITLY

Students don't just move through the school writing process — they can **name it, explain it, and say which stage they're standing in and why**. That's taught directly, revisited in a 60-second check in every lesson, and assessed in Week 10.

### The four stages, in student language

**Mnemonic: FILL · FIND · SHAPE · SHARE** — four stages, four questions, four hand actions, one chant.

| Stage | Our question | What a writer actually does | Hand action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. FILL** 🪣 | *What do I want to say?* | "I collect. I notice, I remember, I wonder, I choose. I'm filling up with things to say — I'm not writing the story yet." | Two cupped hands, like holding water |
| **2. FIND** 🎯 | *Who do I want to say it to?* | "I find my reader. I decide who's going to hear this and what I want them to feel. Then I draft fast and get it all down." | Hands to your eyes like binoculars |
| **3. SHAPE** 🔍 | *How do I want to say it?* | "I read my own writing like an editor. I hold it up against what strong writing does and find what's working and what isn't." | Both hands moulding a ball of clay |
| **4. SHARE** 🎁 | *Is it ready to give away?* | "I ask for help, I recraft it — as many times as it takes — I check the small things, then I give it to my reader." | Open hands, pushed forward, offering |

Stage 1 = Weeks 1–3 · Stage 2 = Weeks 3–6 · Stage 3 = Weeks 7–8 · Stage 4 = Weeks 8–10. Each maps directly onto the school's Stage One–Four wording; FILL/FIND/SHAPE/SHARE is simply the kid-facing handle.

### The chant (call and response, 20 seconds)

> **"Stage one!"** — *What do I want to say — I FILL!*
> **"Stage two!"** — *Who am I saying it to — I FIND!*
> **"Stage three!"** — *How do I want to say it — I SHAPE!*
> **"Stage four!"** — *Is it ready to give away — I SHARE!*

### The feast analogy (for the student who can't explain it)

Writing a piece is like cooking a meal for someone you love. **FILL** — you gather the ingredients; you can't cook with an empty bench. **FIND** — you find out who's coming to dinner, because you'd cook differently for a toddler than for your nana. **SHAPE** — you taste it and adjust it. **SHARE** — you set the table, serve it, and watch their face. The cooking isn't finished when the food is made; it's finished when someone eats it.

### It's a cycle, not a line

Stages 3 and 4 loop: analyse → feedback → recraft → analyse again, as many times as the piece needs. Taught explicitly in Week 9, because students who think writing is a one-way line stop too early.

### The 60-second Process Check (every lesson, straight after the Warm-Up Bridge)

> **Week 1, Monday — teach the spotlight FIRST.** On the very first day the students have never heard of FILL · FIND · SHAPE · SHARE, so running the routine before teaching it makes no sense. That lesson's card in the app carries a red "teach the spotlight first today" banner and puts the spotlight above the routine. The routine is then run for the first time at the end of the spotlight, and from Tuesday onward the normal order applies: routine first, spotlight (on the days there is one) second.

1. *"Hands — which stage are we in?"* Whole class does the action. No talking.
2. *"Name it."* Whole class: the stage word and its question.
3. One student: *"How do you know we're in that stage?"* — the answer must point at what they're actually doing today.

Sixty seconds, fifty times a term. That's what makes it explainable rather than just displayed on a wall.

### The memory techniques behind the design

| Technique | How it shows up |
|---|---|
| **Dual coding** | Every stage has a word, a colour, an icon and an action — four recall hooks instead of one. |
| **Embodied learning** | The hand actions are used daily; the class answers with hands before anyone speaks. |
| **Chant and rhythm** | Call-and-response. Rhythm is why we still remember songs from years ago. |
| **Retrieval practice** | The daily check asks students to recall the stage *before* the teacher names it. Retrieval, not re-reading, builds durable memory. |
| **Spaced repetition** | Full teaching in Weeks 1, 2, 3, 7, 8; deliberate recalls at widening intervals in Weeks 4, 5, 6, 9, 10. |
| **Elaboration & self-explanation** | "Explain it in your own words" · "Teach it to someone at home" · exit tickets asking *why* we're in this stage. |
| **Concrete analogy** | The feast analogy anchors an abstract process to something students already know. |
| **Visible progress** | A four-stage wall strip with a peg the class physically moves — students see where they are, every day. |

### Teaching and recall schedule

| Week | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1** | INTRODUCE all four stages: words, icons, colours, actions, chant, wall strip. Daily check begins. |
| **2** | DEEP DIVE — Stage 1 FILL. What "filling the well" means, and why we don't draft yet. |
| **3** | DEEP DIVE — Stage 2 FIND. Audience, and the handover from FILL. |
| **4** | SPACED RECALL — 60-second quick-fire, no notes. |
| **5** | ELABORATION — explain the process to a partner in your own words, using the feast analogy. |
| **6** | SPACED RECALL + move the peg, with a justification out loud. |
| **7** | DEEP DIVE — Stage 3 SHAPE. Reading your own writing as an editor. |
| **8** | DEEP DIVE — Stage 4 SHARE. Feedback and recrafting in cycles. |
| **9** | THE CYCLE — students draw the process as a loop, from memory. |
| **10** | ASSESS — "Explain our writing process to someone who has never heard of it." |

### Week 10 assessment

> **"Explain our writing process to someone who has never heard of it. Name all four stages, say what a writer actually does in each one, and give one example from your own narrative this term."**

A strong answer: names all four stages in order with their questions · explains in their own words what a writer *does* in each · gives a real example from their own piece for at least two stages · knows that Stages 3 and 4 repeat in cycles until the piece is ready. Spoken to a partner first (60 seconds), then written into the Week 10 reflection. **"Process"** is also a row on the single-point rubric.

# WEEK 1 — "Every Person Is a Story"
**Stage 1: Immersion & Idea Generation** · Essential Question: *What makes a moment worth writing about?*

**Warm-Up skills this week:** Mon — Tier 2 descriptive adjectives · Tue — nouns & proper nouns · Wed — simple sentence structure · Thu — proofreading capitals & full stops.

**Weekly Writing Prompt:** *"Think of a moment you have never forgotten — even though it only lasted a few minutes."*

**Question Ladder**
1. How old were you? Where were you standing?
2. Who else was there — and what were they doing?
3. What is the one picture from that moment still stuck in your head?
4. Why do you think your brain decided to keep this one?

**Word & Starter Bank:** vivid · ordinary · flicker · sudden · unforgettable · ache · fierce · still · rushed · quiet
*Starters:* "I still remember the moment when…" · "It only took a second, but…" · "Nobody else noticed, but I did."

**Still Stuck? (reveal)**
- *Borrow this:* the day something you owned broke, got lost, or was given away.
- *Image prompt:* a photo of an empty playground at dusk.
- *Talk it out:* tell your partner about the last time you laughed so hard it hurt. Now — what happened just before that?

**Mentor texts:** any short small-moment narrative you have to hand. Suggested NZ options: a short excerpt from *Whale Rider* (Ihimaera), a School Journal small-moment story, or a picture book like *Grandad's Wheelies* or *The Red Rock*. One text, revisited all week, beats four texts skimmed.

### Monday — Launch: The Moment That Stuck
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "In this morning's warm-up you upgraded plain adjectives. Watch how a real writer chooses one perfect word instead of three ordinary ones."
- **I Do (7):** Tell (don't read) a 90-second true story from your own life — a small one. A dropped ice cream. A pet that ran away. Then think aloud: *"Notice I didn't tell you about my whole weekend. I told you about ninety seconds. That's what a small-moment story is. And notice which bit you leaned in for — that's the heart."*
- **We Do (7):** Read the mentor text aloud. Class hunts: where exactly is the *heart* of this story — the sentence where everything turns? Mark it together. Ask: how long did this story actually last, in real time?
- **You Do (8):** **Jotter launch.** Every student gets/decorates a jotter. First task: the *Moment Seeds* list — write 5 one-line moments you've never forgotten. Just lines, not stories. ("The night the power went out." "When Koro let me drive the ute.")
- **Close (2):** Three students read one seed aloud. No commentary. "Tomorrow we find out which seeds have a story hiding inside them."

### Tuesday — Build Together: What Makes a Story Worth Telling
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Nouns name specific things — and specific names are what make a story feel real. Not 'a dog'. *Rusty.*"
- **I Do (5):** Show two versions of the same moment on the board: one vague ("We went to the beach and it was fun"), one specific ("The tide had gone out so far the sand looked like it went on forever"). Think aloud about what the second one gives the reader.
- **We Do (8):** Class builds the **"Worth Telling" chart** together. Teacher scribes. Prompt: what did our mentor text have that made us care? Steer towards: *something changed · someone felt something · it happened in a real place · there was a problem or a surprise.* This chart stays on the wall all term.
- **You Do (8):** Students test their 5 seeds against the chart. Tick the ones that pass. Add 3 more seeds now that they know what to look for.
- **Close (2):** "Whose seed changed after today?"

### Wednesday — Stretch: The Jotter Habit
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Subject + verb + object. Every noticing you write today can be that simple — a jotter isn't for perfect sentences."
- **I Do (5):** Model your own jotter page on the visualiser: messy, dated, ungraded. Three noticings from your walk to the car. Say clearly: *"I never mark your jotter. It's yours. It's where writers keep the raw stuff."*
- **We Do (7):** **Noticing walk** (if possible, physically — 5 minutes outside; otherwise, out the window). Class collects noticings aloud; teacher scribes 10 on the board without editing them.
- **You Do (9):** Jotter: *Noticings, Wonderings, Feelings* — three columns, three entries each from today. Plus the **For Your Jotter** faith prompt: *"Where did I see love — aroha — today? Where was it missing?"*
- **Close (2):** Preview: "Tomorrow you meet a story that's only 200 words long — and it will still get you."

### Thursday — Write: Reading Like a Writer
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "You proofread for capitals and full stops this morning. Today, watch where a real writer *breaks* those rules on purpose."
- **I Do (6):** Reread the mentor text's opening 3 sentences only. Think aloud: *"Why did she start here? She didn't start with 'One day'. She started in the middle."*
- **We Do (6):** **Three-Level Guide (quick version)** on the mentor text — one question at each level: *On the page:* what happened? *Between the lines:* how did the character feel and how do we know? *Beyond:* what does this writer want us to understand about people?
- **You Do (9):** Students pick their strongest seed and write a **"first telling"** — 5 minutes, no stopping, just get the moment down. Fast and rough on purpose. This is not the draft.
- **Close (2):** No sharing today. "Close the jotter. Let it sit."

### Friday — Share & Reflect: Story Circle
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** Recap: this week's warm-ups gave us better adjectives, specific nouns, clean sentences. All three are story tools.
- **I Do (3):** Model how to *listen* to a story: eyes up, no interrupting, one question at the end.
- **We Do (5):** Teacher tells one more short true story — this time deliberately dull. Class diagnoses: what was missing?
- **You Do (12):** **Story Circle** in groups of 4. Each student *tells* (not reads) their moment in 60 seconds. Listeners' only job: say back the one image they can still see.
- **Close (3):** Quick class reflection: "Which of your moments do you want to actually write? You don't have to decide yet."

# WEEK 2 — "Filling the Well"
**Stage 1: Imaging, senses, and choosing the moment** · Essential Question: *How do writers make a reader feel something?*

**Warm-Up skills this week:** Mon — commas in lists · Tue — action vs state verbs · Wed — expanding sentences with adjectives · Thu — fixing run-on sentences.

**Weekly Writing Prompt:** *"Take your moment and put me inside it. What could I see, hear, smell, feel if I was standing next to you?"*

**Question Ladder**
1. Close your eyes. What's the first thing you see in this memory?
2. What sound was in the background that you'd forgotten?
3. What could you feel — on your skin, in your stomach?
4. If this moment had a colour and a temperature, what would they be?

**Word & Starter Bank:** damp · sharp · humming · flickering · thick · bitter · echoing · scorching · gritty · hushed
*Starters:* "The air smelled like…" · "Somewhere behind me, I could hear…" · "My hands were…"

**Still Stuck? (reveal)**
- *Borrow this:* the first time you were somewhere brand new — a new school, a new house, someone else's family.
- *Image prompt:* a kitchen at night with one light on.
- *Talk it out:* describe your bedroom to a partner with your eyes shut. Now do that to your memory.

### Monday — Launch: Imaging
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Commas in lists — perfect, because today we're listing what our senses caught."
- **I Do (7):** **Guided visualisation.** Students shut eyes; you walk them slowly into one of your own memories, naming the senses aloud one at a time. Then show the 4 sentences you wrote from it. Say: *"I didn't invent that detail. I went back and looked."*
- **We Do (6):** Class chooses one shared memory (school swimming sports, the fire drill, a wet-day lunchtime). Build a **Five Senses Map** together on the board.
- **You Do (8):** Students do the same for their chosen moment — a five-box sensory map in the jotter. Boxes may be words, not sentences.
- **Close (2):** "Whose sense box surprised you the most?"

### Tuesday — Build Together: Show, Don't Tell
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Action verbs versus state verbs — this is exactly today's lesson. 'I was scared' is a state. 'My hands shook' is an action."
- **I Do (5):** Model the swap. Write "I was really excited." Cross it out. Think aloud through three replacements; land on *"I couldn't stop bouncing on my toes."*
- **We Do (9):** **Show-Don't-Tell relay.** Teacher gives a telling sentence, class fires back showing versions: *He was angry · She was nervous · It was boring · They were proud.* Scribe the best of each — this becomes a class chart.
- **You Do (7):** Students find every "telling" emotion word in their first telling from Week 1 Thursday and rewrite two of them as showing.
- **Close (2):** Two volunteers read a before and after.

### Wednesday — Stretch: Stories We Inherit
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "You expanded sentences with adjectives this morning. Careful — today we're after *precise*, not *more*."
- **I Do (5):** Tell a story from your own family that was told to you — a grandparent's story. Connect: **whakapapa** and **kōrero tuku iho**; our stories are handed down. *Deuteronomy 6:6–9.*
- **We Do (7):** Class discussion: what makes a family story survive fifty years? Chart it: *it's funny · someone was brave · it explains why we do something.*
- **You Do (9):** Jotter: **"A story from my whānau"** — one line of a story someone in their family tells. (Homework option: ask someone at home tonight.) Plus: *Where is my tūrangawaewae — the place where my story stands?*
- **Close (2):** Preview Friday: "You'll choose your moment for the term."

### Thursday — Write: Freewrite the Feeling
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Run-on sentences — sometimes in a rush of feeling, that's exactly what your brain does. Today we let it, then we fix it later."
- **I Do (4):** Model a 3-minute freewrite on the visualiser. Pen doesn't stop. Show the mess. *"Real writers' first pages look like this."*
- **We Do (4):** Set the rules together: no crossing out, no stopping, no worrying about spelling, keep going even if you write "I don't know what to write."

# WEEK 4 — "Getting In: Openings & Setting"
**Stage 2: Drafting** · Essential Question: *How do writers make a reader want the next sentence?*

**Warm-Up skills this week:** Mon — apostrophes for possession · Tue — pronouns · Wed — topic sentences · Thu — pronoun–antecedent agreement.

**Weekly Writing Prompt:** *"Start in the middle of something. Drop your reader into your moment with no explaining."*

**Question Ladder**
1. What is happening in the very first second of your moment?
2. Could you begin with a sound? A line of speech? An action?
3. What does the reader absolutely need to know in the first three sentences — and what can wait?
4. What's the *last* sentence of your opening — the one that makes them keep going?

**Word & Starter Bank:** crouched · sprawled · rattling · looming · faded · cluttered · narrow · glaring · muddy · deserted
*Starters:* "The first thing I noticed was…" · "Nobody was supposed to be there." · "It started with a sound."

**Still Stuck? (reveal)**
- *Borrow this opening shape:* start with one line of dialogue someone said to you, then step back and explain where you were.
- *Image prompt:* a hallway with a door slightly open.
- *Talk it out:* say your first sentence to a partner three different ways. Keep the one they reacted to.

### Monday — Launch: Four Ways to Open
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Apostrophes for possession — you'll need them: *Dad's ute, Nana's kitchen, my brother's fault.*"

- **We Do (6):** Class rewrites a deliberately weak opening ("One day I went to the beach with my family and we had a really good time") in each of the four shapes.
- **You Do (7):** Students write **three different openings** to their own piece. Not one — three.
- **Close (2):** Partner picks the strongest of the three. Writer doesn't argue; writer just listens.

### Tuesday — Build Together: Setting That Works
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Pronouns this morning. Watch out — if you write 'he' too early, your reader doesn't know who he is yet."
- **I Do (5):** Model the **three-detail rule**: pick three specific details of a place, not ten. Show the difference by reading a bloated setting paragraph and then the trimmed version.
- **We Do (9):** Joint construction: build a setting paragraph for a place the whole class knows (the school field on a hot day). Teacher scribes; class supplies details; class then *cuts* it down to three.
- **You Do (7):** Students draft their setting into their piece, choosing exactly three details from their Week 2 sensory map.
- **Close (2):** "Read your three details. Could any of them belong to any old place? Then it's not specific enough yet."

### Wednesday — Stretch: Drafting Fast & Furious
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Topic sentences this morning — the first sentence of each new paragraph tells the reader where they now are."
- **I Do (4):** Explain the rule for today: **fast drafting**. No rubbing out, no perfecting, leave a blank if a word won't come, keep moving. Model 60 seconds of it yourself.
- **We Do (4):** Class agrees on the signals: what to do when stuck (draw a squiggle and keep going), when you finish a section (start the next), when the timer stops (draw a line, don't tidy).
- **You Do (14):** **Sustained drafting.** Music off, timer visible, teacher writing alongside for the first half then circulating silently. Longest single writing block of the term so far.
- **Close (1):** Word count noted in the margin. No sharing. Protect the flow.

### Thursday — Write: Independent Drafting + First Conferences
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Pronoun agreement — as you reread today, check every 'they' and 'it' actually has an owner."
- **I Do (4):** Model **rereading before writing**: always read your last paragraph before you add a new one. Show yourself doing it.
- **We Do (3):** Quick shared reminder of the plan boxes; students point to which box they're in today.
- **You Do (14):** Independent drafting. Teacher runs **4–5 two-minute conferences** with the students most likely to stall (identified from the gallery walk and Week 3 rehearsal). Conference script: *"Read me your last sentence. What happens next? Say it out loud. Now write exactly what you just said."*
- **Close (2):** "Everyone finish the sentence you're on. Not the paragraph. The sentence."

### Friday — Share & Reflect: Author's Chair (Openings)
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** This week's warm-ups sharpened possession, pronouns and topic sentences — all three show up in a strong opening.
- **I Do (3):** Set the Author's Chair rules: the writer reads, we listen, we clap, then two people say one specific thing that worked. No suggestions today — praise only.
- **We Do (4):** Model with your own opening, taking praise gracefully.
- **You Do (13):** **Openings-only Author's Chair.** 6–8 students read *just their opening* (30 seconds each). Fast rotation. Everyone reads over the course of the term; keep a tally so the same four kids don't dominate.
- **Close (3):** "Which opening from today made you want to hear the rest? Tell that person on the way out."

# WEEK 5 — "The Heart of the Moment"
**Stage 2: Drafting** · Essential Question: *How do I slow down the part that matters most?*

**Warm-Up skills this week:** Mon — exclamation & question marks · Tue — degrees of comparison · Wed — sentence variety (short vs long) · Thu — peer-checking punctuation.

**Weekly Writing Prompt:** *"Find the ten seconds that everything hinges on. Now stretch them across half a page."*

**Question Ladder**
1. What is the exact second the moment turns?
2. What did your body do — before your brain caught up?
3. Did anyone say anything? What were the exact words?
4. What were you thinking that you did *not* say out loud?

**Word & Starter Bank:** froze · lurched · slammed · whispered · muttered · gasped · trembling · thundering · churning · pounding
*Starters:* "That was when…" · "I couldn't move." · "Nobody said anything for a long time."

**Still Stuck? (reveal)**
- *Borrow this:* write the moment in slow motion, one body part at a time — hands, then stomach, then eyes.
- *Image prompt:* a smashed cup on a wooden floor.
- *Talk it out:* act out the ten seconds with a partner, then write only what a camera would have caught.

### Monday — Launch: Slow Motion
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Exclamation marks! Warning: in strong writing they're rare. The tension should come from the words, not the punctuation."
- **I Do (8):** Show two versions of the same climax: one that covers it in a sentence, one stretched over eight. Think aloud about **zooming in** — heartbeat, hands, breath, the one thing you were staring at.
- **We Do (6):** Class stretches a fast sentence together: *"I fell off my bike."* → eight sentences, scribed on the board.
- **You Do (7):** Students find the ten-second heart of their own moment and stretch it. Minimum six sentences on ten seconds.
- **Close (2):** One reader. Class names the slowest sentence.

### Tuesday — Build Together: Dialogue That Does a Job
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Comparisons this morning — *bigger, biggest, more frightening.* Dialogue is where kids' comparisons come alive."
- **I Do (6):** Model dialogue punctuation explicitly on the board — speech marks, comma before the closing mark, new line for a new speaker, capital for the first spoken word. Then model *choosing* what a character says: real speech is short, interrupted, unfinished.
- **We Do (8):** Joint construction of a 4-line exchange between two people in an argument over something small. Class supplies the words; teacher punctuates aloud, narrating every mark. Then cut every "said happily/angrily/sadly" and replace with an action beat.
- **You Do (7):** Students add one real exchange to their draft — 2–4 lines only, punctuated correctly.
- **Close (2):** Two writers read their dialogue aloud with a partner playing the other voice.

### Wednesday — Stretch: Sentence Length as a Tool
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Short versus long sentences was this morning's warm-up — this lesson is that warm-up, put to work."
- **I Do (5):** Read a tense passage aloud and let the class hear the short sentences hitting. *"He waited. Nothing. Then the door moved."* Name the technique: long sentences to build, short ones to strike.
- **We Do (8):** Class rewrites a long, breathless paragraph by breaking it into a long–long–short pattern. Read both aloud; ask what changed in the *sound*.
- **You Do (8):** Students find their tensest paragraph and deliberately place one very short sentence in it.
- **Close (2):** "Read your short sentence to your neighbour. Just that one."

### Thursday — Write: Independent Drafting
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "You peer-checked punctuation this morning. Today you're the only checker — so read it out loud in your head as you go."
- **I Do (3):** Model getting unstuck mid-draft: reread the plan, say the next bit aloud, write it.
- **We Do (3):** Students each name the plan box they'll finish today, aloud, quickly round the room.
- **You Do (15):** Sustained drafting. Teacher takes a **small-group workshop (4–5 students)** at the back table on whatever the drafts show is needed most — usually dialogue punctuation or paragraphing.
- **Close (2):** "Tomorrow you'll read your hardest paragraph to two people. Choose it now and mark it."

### Friday — Share & Reflect: Trios
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** The week's warm-ups — punctuation, comparison, sentence variety — are the three things listeners will hear today.
- **I Do (3):** Model the trio protocol: read · listeners hold the picture · one listener says the image they saw, the other asks one question.
- **We Do (4):** Practise once as a whole class on your writing.
- **You Do (13):** **Trios.** Each writer reads their marked paragraph. Listeners respond with the protocol. Rotate.
- **Close (3):** Jotter line: *"One thing I'm going to change on Monday is…"*

# WEEK 8 — "The Helping Circle"

### Friday — Share & Reflect: Before and After
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** This week's warm-ups were all detail-adders. Every one of them is a revision tool.
- **I Do (3):** Model reading a before and after aloud, and naming the move used.
- **We Do (4):** Class names what they notice about the change.
- **You Do (13):** **Before/After share** in pairs then four volunteers to the class: read the old version, then the new one, and name the move (add/cut/reorder/replace).
- **Close (3):** Jotter: *"The feedback that helped me most was… because…"*

# WEEK 9 — "Recraft, Edit, Best Copy"
**Stage 4: Revise and recraft** · Essential Question: *What does it look like to make my writing better rather than just neater?*

**Warm-Up skills this week:** Mon — onomatopoeia & sound words · Tue — coordinating vs subordinating conjunctions · Wed — paragraph cohesion · Thu — removing repetition.

**Weekly Writing Prompt:** *No new prompt — this week is second draft and best copy.*

### Monday — Launch: The Real Second Draft
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Onomatopoeia — *crack, hiss, thud.* One well-placed sound word can carry a whole paragraph. One is plenty; five is a cartoon."
- **I Do (6):** Model rewriting a paragraph properly — not tinkering, but rewriting from scratch with the goal in mind. Show both versions side by side.
- **We Do (5):** Class decides which of two versions of a paragraph is stronger and articulates *why* using criteria language.
- **You Do (10):** Students begin the **second draft** of their strongest-needing section, applying revision plan change #1.
- **Close (2):** "Who rewrote rather than patched? What was that like?"

### Tuesday — Build Together: Cohesion & Flow
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Coordinating and subordinating conjunctions — *and/but/so* versus *because/although/while.* The second set makes writing sound older."
- **I Do (5):** Model reading a piece aloud to test flow, marking every place the ear stumbles.
- **We Do (7):** Class smooths a choppy paragraph together with linking words and varied openers.
- **You Do (9):** Students apply revision changes #2 and #3 and read their whole piece aloud in a whisper, marking stumbles.
- **Close (2):** "How many stumbles did you find? That number is your to-do list."

### Wednesday — Stretch: The Edit (GPS)
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Paragraph cohesion this morning — today we check the same thing on our own page."
- **I Do (5):** Model the **editing pass order**, one pass per thing, on the visualiser: (1) capitals & full stops, (2) speech punctuation, (3) tense, (4) spelling of words I'm unsure of.
- **We Do (6):** Class edits a short passage using the same four passes, one pass at a time.
- **You Do (10):** Students edit their own piece in four separate passes. Word cards / dictionaries out. **Peer edit swap** for the final pass only.
- **Close (2):** "Editing isn't the boring bit — it's the respect bit. *Colossians 3:23.*"

### Thursday — Write: Best Copy in Books
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Repetition got removed this morning. Final check: did you start three sentences in a row the same way?"
- **I Do (3):** Model presentation expectations for the handwritten best copy: title, date, margins, legible joined handwriting, paragraphs indented.
- **We Do (2):** Class agrees on the presentation checklist and it goes on the board.
- **You Do (18):** **Best copy written into writing books.** Quiet, calm, music optional. Handwriting stamina across a multi-paragraph text is itself a Phase 2 outcome — name that to them.
- **Close (2):** "If you're not finished, that's fine — finish tomorrow. Nobody rushes a best copy."

### Friday — Share & Reflect: Finishing & Reading Aloud
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** This week's warm-ups were the polish tools: sound, conjunctions, cohesion, repetition.
- **I Do (2):** Model reading a finished piece aloud well — pace, pauses, looking up at the end.
- **We Do (4):** Class practises "the reading voice" on one shared paragraph.
- **You Do (14):** Finish best copies; those finished **rehearse reading aloud** in pairs for next week's celebration, and give each other one tip on delivery.
- **Close (3):** "Next week: Microsoft Word, and the celebration. Bring your best reading voice."

# WEEK 10 — "Publish & Celebrate"
**Stage 4: Publication** · Essential Question: *What has this term made me, as a writer?*

**Warm-Up skills this week:** Mon–Thu are Term 1 review sessions (punctuation, grammar, sentence types, full-passage edit) — perfect timing, because this week students proofread a typed piece.

### Monday — Launch: Typing as a Publishing Skill
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "This morning's warm-up reviewed every punctuation mark from Term 1. You're about to need all of them."

- **We Do (5):** Class walks through save location and filename convention together, out loud.
- **You Do (10):** Students begin typing their published piece from the book copy.

- **We Do (5):** Class agrees the presentation standard for the published set: title, author name, one clear font, paragraphs separated.
- **You Do (11):** Continue typing and formatting.
- **Close (2):** "Who found a mistake while typing that they'd missed on paper? That's why we type second, not first."

### Wednesday — Stretch: Final Proof & Print
- **Warm-Up Bridge (2):** "Sentence types review — read your typed piece and check you haven't got ten sentences of exactly the same shape."

- **You Do (14):** Final proof, partner check, print (or save to the class folder / portfolio). Illustrations or a title-page image optional for those finished.

- **We Do (2):** The audience's job is named: listen, then one person names one specific thing they'll remember.

- **Close (1):** Name the value out loud: this is **Aroha** — we gave our stories to each other.

### Friday — Reflect: What Kind of Writer Am I Becoming?

### The whole term's formal marking load

### Appendix 1 — Single-Point Rubric: Term 1 Narrative

Student name: ________________  Title: ________________

| Success Criteria (what a strong narrative does) | ✓ | Glow — evidence it's working | Grow → one next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ideas** — tells one small moment, not a whole day, and it clearly mattered to the writer | | | |
| **Opening** — drops the reader into the moment (action, dialogue, sound or image), no long wind-up | | | |
| **Setting** — three specific details place the reader somewhere real | | | |
| **Showing** — feelings are shown through action, body and speech, not named | | | |
| **The heart** — the key moment is slowed down and stretched | | | |
| **Dialogue** — at least one real exchange, punctuated with speech marks and commas | | | |
| **Structure** — paragraphs move the reader (new time / place / speaker / focus) | | | |
| **Language** — precise verbs and at least one deliberate simile or metaphor | | | |
| **Sentences** — a range of lengths and openers, used for effect | | | |
| **Ending** — lands rather than stops; a reflective final line | | | |
| **Accuracy** — capitals, full stops, tense consistency, known spellings | | | |
| **Audience** — a reader outside our class could follow and feel it | | |
| **Process** — can name our four writing stages and explain which one they are in, and why | | | |

**Overall:** ☐ Working towards ☐ Meeting Phase 2 expectations ☐ Exceeding
**Glow (one sentence):**
**Grow (one next step, written as "Next time, try…"):**

*Teacher time-saver:* only fill Glow and Grow. The ticks do the rest. Grow must be **one** step — a list of six is a list nobody acts on.

**Student-facing version:** identical criteria, worded as "I can…" statements, used for self-assessment in Week 7 and peer feedback in Week 8. Same words, three uses — that's what makes the standard stick.

### Appendix 2 — The Helping Circle (Term 1 narrative version)

**Groups of 4. Five minutes per writer. Timer visible.**

1. **The writer reads aloud** — no introduction, no apology, no "it's not very good". Listeners do not interrupt.
2. **The writer asks their question** — the specific one they wrote in Week 7 Friday.
3. **Two stars** — each listener names one specific thing that worked, using criteria language.
4. **One wish** — each listener offers one actionable next step.
5. **The writer says thank you** and writes it down. **No defending, no explaining.** The writer decides later what to use.

**Genre-specific listener prompts (put these on the table as a card):**

*Stars — look for:*
- The image I can still see is…
- I knew exactly how you felt when you wrote…
- The sentence that made me want to keep listening was…
- Your ending made me think…

*Wishes — choose one:*
- I wanted to be *in* the moment more at the part where…
- I got confused about who was speaking when…
- You told me you felt ____ — could you show me instead?
- The beginning took a while to get going — could you start at…?
- I wanted to know what you were thinking just before…

**Ground rules poster:** *We talk about the writing, never the writer. Every wish must be something a person could actually do tomorrow. Everyone gets their full five minutes.*

### Appendix 3 — Exemplar Bank

**Exemplar A — Strong (WAGOLL), ~200 words**

> The screen door banged twice behind me before I realised Rusty wasn't following.
>
> It was the kind of afternoon where the driveway shimmered and the neighbour's radio drifted over the fence, playing a song nobody liked. I stood on the hot concrete in bare feet and called his name once. Then twice. The gate was open.
>
> "Did you shut it?" Mum's voice came from the kitchen window, already sharp.
>
> "I thought I did."
>
> I ran to the corner, and everything got very loud and very quiet at the same time. My heart was doing something strange in my throat. A car went past. Another one. I couldn't remember how to breathe properly, so I just kept running, past the letterboxes, past the church, past the dairy where he sometimes waited for chips.
>
> He was sitting outside it. Tail going. Absolutely delighted with himself.
>
> I dropped onto the footpath and put my face into his neck, which smelled like dust and dog and warm afternoon, and I didn't care that Mrs Petersen was watching from the shop door.
>
> I still check that gate twice. Every single time.

*What to point at:* opens mid-action · three setting details, not ten · shows fear through body and breath, never names it · dialogue punctuated and short · paragraph break at the moment of the turn · sentence length shortens at the tense part · circles back to the gate at the end.

**Exemplar B — Weaker (for diagnosis in Week 7), ~150 words**

> One day I lost my dog and it was really scary and sad. It was a hot day. My dog is called Rusty and he is a brown dog and he is 4 years old and he likes chips and going for walks and sleeping on my bed even though he isn't allowed. Anyway I went outside and he wasn't there so I was really worried and scared and upset. My mum said did you shut the gate and I said I don't know. So then I ran down the road and I looked everywhere and I couldn't find him and I was really scared. Then I found him at the dairy and I was really happy. Then we went home and had tea. It was a good day in the end. I love my dog so much he is the best dog ever.

*What the class will find:* tells feelings instead of showing them ("really scared" three times) · a whole paragraph of dog facts that don't serve the story · no paragraphing · run-on sentences with "and… and… and" · dialogue not punctuated · ends by stopping ("then we had tea") · no specific sensory detail.

*Teaching note:* always analyse the weaker one **second**, and never present it as a real child's work. "I wrote this one badly on purpose — find my mistakes" is safe and enormous fun.

**Exemplar C — A mid-range piece** is worth adding once you have real student work from this first run of the unit. A mid-range exemplar is the most useful of the three, because most of the class is standing right there.

### Appendix 4 — Differentiation

| If a student… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Can't find a moment | Give the Layer 4 "borrow this" scenario without ceremony. A borrowed starting point is not cheating; it's a scaffold. |
| Freezes at the blank page | Oral first, always. Let them dictate the first paragraph to a partner or a device, then copy it. |
| Writes fluently but shallowly | Set a stretch goal: the ten-second heart must fill half a page, and the piece must include one deliberate metaphor they can justify. |
| Struggles with transcription | Reduce the volume, never the thinking. A strong 150-word piece beats a laboured 400. Scribing or speech-to-text for the draft, handwritten best copy shortened. |
| Finishes early, every week | Second piece from a different Week 1 seed, or become a **Helping Circle facilitator** for a group that needs one. |
| Is reluctant to share aloud | Reading to one partner counts. Author's Chair can be "someone else reads my piece" — the writing is still published. |
