# TERM 4 — WRITING FOCUS UNIT PLAN
## Poetry & Reflective Writing: *"Every Word Counts"*
### Years 5–6 (Ages 9–12) · 10 weeks · ~25 min per lesson, 5 days per week

Every lesson exists in full lesson-plan form in the **Writing Focus** app (WALT, NZC link and tags, the daily Process Check, scripted I Do / We Do / You Do). This document is the planning overview.

> **Two decisions you can change.** (1) The anthology is published for an **end-of-year celebration** — a portfolio evening, a prize-giving slot, or a class-to-whānau reading in the last week. (2) Term 4 includes the **year-in-review portfolio task**, because it is the natural close to four terms of the same process and takes only Weeks 9–10 to do properly. If your end-of-year event falls earlier, move Weeks 9–10 forward and let the final week be a quieter reflection week after the performance.

**Two published outcomes this term:**
1. **The anthology** — every student polishes **three poems**, handwritten as best copies (Week 9), typed for a class anthology (Week 10), and one is read aloud at the celebration.
2. **The year-in-review reflection** — students lay out four terms of their own published writing, choose the piece they're proudest of, and write honestly about how they've changed as a writer. It goes in the portfolio and on to next year's teacher.

## PART A — ACS UNIT PLAN FRONT MATTER

### UNDERSTAND

**Universal Concept:** *Attention and Gratitude — Noticing Is the Beginning of Everything*
Attention · Wonder · Gratitude · Precision · Impact — a few words, well chosen, can change how someone sees.

**Big Idea (Writing):**
Poets don't have better ideas than everyone else. They look for longer, and then they take away every word that isn't earning its place.

**Throughline Question:**
*"What did I notice this year that nobody else did — and can I make it land in ten words?"*

**Biblical Perspective**

| Element | This term |
|---|---|
| Framework | **Creation and Restoration** — attention as worship. To look closely at an ordinary thing and find it worth writing about is a way of honouring the One who made it. |
| Attributes of God | God as **Maker of small things** — the lilies, the sparrow, the hairs of your head. Nothing is beneath notice. |
| Principles | Learning about God and His plan for creation · Developing faith and character · Valuing and serving others |
| Motto | **Soli Deo Gloria** — the year ends with gratitude, and gratitude begins with noticing. |

**Anchor Value: PĀNGA — Impact: bringing God's Kingdom to earth.**
Impact isn't only loud. Twenty-five students who have learned to notice, and to say what they noticed so it lands in someone else, change a room. The celebration frames the year's writing as a gift: *these words are yours to give away.*

**Key Scriptures**

- *Psalm 8:3–4* — "When I consider your heavens… what is mankind that you are mindful of them?" (Week 1: noticing as worship).
- *Matthew 6:28* — "Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow" (Week 2: look closely at small things).
- *Philippians 4:8* — "Whatever is true, noble, right, pure, lovely… think on these things" (Week 5: what we choose to notice).
- *Psalm 19:14* — "May the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be pleasing to you" (Week 8: reading aloud).
- *1 Thessalonians 5:18* — "Give thanks in all circumstances" (Week 10: the year's last word).

**Mātauranga Māori / Te Tiriti connection:**
**Whakataukī** — the Māori proverb tradition, where enormous meaning is carried in very few words, and where the image (a tree, a bird, a river) does the work that explanation would ruin. Students meet whakataukī as poetry: *"He iti te mokoroa, nāna i kakati te kahikatea"* — the grub is small, yet it fells the kahikatea. Also **taonga tuku iho**: the anthology is made as something handed on, and the year finishes with students literally writing the process down for the students who follow them.

### KNOW — Curriculum Links

**NZC English, Phase 2 (Years 4–6) — Writing**

*Writing craft — language features and devices*
- **use figurative language such as simile and metaphor to purposefully create vivid images and effects to engage the reader**

*Writing craft — word choice*
- select and use words and phrases that clearly express where and when things happen
- vocabulary chosen deliberately for effect, including connotation and precision

*Sentence structures and punctuation*
- use a range of sentence structures and types to suit purpose and enhance impact
- **demonstrate understanding of incomplete (fragment) sentences** — and, in poetry, use them deliberately

*Composition — audience, purpose and task*
- identify the audience and purpose for a text
- understand that different audiences may have a very different response to the same text (in poetry, this is a feature rather than a fault)

*Writing processes*
- Revising: reread at word, line, stanza and whole-text level; revise by **replacing and removing**; use peer feedback
- Editing: edit using known punctuation; use a dictionary for unknown spellings
- Planning & goals: **set and work towards specific writing goals based on reflection on their own writing content and processes**

*Transcription*
- handwrite with increasing stamina and fluency while maintaining legibility — assessed on the anthology best copies

**NZC English, Phase 2 — Reading**
- respond to texts by sharing opinions and personal thoughts and feelings about ideas in texts
- determine how ideas are conveyed through text features and **visual elements** — in poetry, line breaks, stanza gaps and white space
- acknowledge others' responses and respond respectfully to differences

**NZC English, Phase 2 — Oral Language**
- use precise vocabulary
- vary sentence structure and length to create effect and support meaning
- engage listeners actively — here, by reading a poem aloud so it lands

**Knowledge outcomes — students will know that…**
1. A poem is proof that somebody looked. It starts in attention, not in inspiration.
2. Your first three noticings are everyone's — the good ones arrive after a minute of looking.
3. A comparison you've heard before isn't yours. Generate five; the fifth is usually the true one.
4. Concrete nouns and precise verbs carry a poem; most adjectives don't.
5. A line break is a choice: the word at the end of a line gets noticed, and the gap between stanzas is a silence that does work.
6. Cutting is the main way a poem improves — usually by about a third.
7. A poem should stop before it explains itself. The real ending is often one line earlier than the one you wrote.
8. A poem isn't finished until someone has heard it.

**Possible misconceptions**
- ⚠ "Poems have to rhyme." (They can. But if the rhyme is choosing your words, the rhyme is writing the poem.)
- ⚠ "Poetry means beautiful language." (It means *exact* language. "Legs locked" beats "gorgeous shimmering wings".)
- ⚠ "Longer is better." (In this genre, almost the reverse.)
- ⚠ "I have to explain what my poem means." (You don't — and being asked to is a signal, not a duty.)
- ⚠ "Line breaks go where the line reaches the edge of the page." (They go where your voice pauses or where a word deserves attention.)
- ⚠ "A poem about something small is a small poem." (The shell, the orange, the hands — small subjects carry the biggest poems.)

**Essential Questions (used as WALTs)**
1. What does a poem actually do? (Week 1)
2. How do I make a comparison that is mine and nobody else's? (Week 2)
3. Where should the lines break, and who is going to hear this? (Week 3)
4. How do I make a poem sound like something, not just mean something? (Week 5)
5. What can I take out? (Week 7)
6. How have I changed as a writer this year? (Weeks 9–10)

## PART C — THE WEEKLY RHYTHM

| Day | Role | Balance |
|---|---|---|
| **Monday** | **Launch** — new focus through a model poem or demonstration | I Do–heavy |
| **Tuesday** | **Build together** — the class makes an example jointly | We Do–heavy |
| **Wednesday** | **Stretch** — a harder application of the same skill | We Do → You Do |
| **Thursday** | **Write** — students apply it to their own poems | You Do–heavy |
| **Friday** | **Share and reflect** — circles, readings, and the two-readings rule | Student voice |

**Every 25-minute lesson:** Warm-Up Bridge (2) → Process Check (1) → I Do (5–8) → We Do (5–8) → You Do (7–10) → Close (1–2).

**The two-readings rule:** every poem read in this room — mentor text, exemplar or student work — is read **twice**, with a **five-second silence** afterwards. It is taught in Week 1 Friday and never dropped. A poem read once has not been heard, and a response given instantly is a response to the last line only.

**A note on this term's warm-ups:** Term 4's Daily Warm-Up is a whole-year review sweeping all four genres, so several bridges deliberately note the contrast ("that's a persuasive tool — watch how a poem uses it differently"). Several land beautifully anyway: precise verbs (W1), pathetic fallacy (W2), sentence length for tension (W5), **fragments for deliberate effect** (W7), and the year-review and portfolio warm-ups in Weeks 9–10, which sit directly alongside the reflection task.

## PART D — THE IDEA-SUPPORT SYSTEM (identical every week)

- **Layer 1 — The Prompt** · **Layer 2 — Question Ladder** · **Layer 3 — Word & Starter Bank** · **Layer 4 — "Still Stuck?"** (borrow / image / talk it out).

**Printable student worksheet.** Every week's prompt prints as a two-page student planning sheet (**📝 Print student worksheet** in the app) — prompt, question ladder with writing space, word bank as tick-boxes, sentence starters, a plan/notes page, a box for the first lines, the "only if you're stuck" layer and a self-check.

**The Term 4 "still stuck" answer is different from every other term's:** don't ask a stuck student for an *idea* — hand them an *object*. A leaf, a key, a shoe on the desk, and two minutes to write only what they can see. It works almost every time, because in this genre the blank page is a looking problem, not a thinking one.

## PART D2 — TEACHING THE WRITING PROCESS: THE FOURTH GENRE

Same four stages, fourth term: **FILL · FIND · SHAPE · SHARE**. Poetry is the strongest possible test of the process, because it looks least like the other three — and it still needs all four stages.

| Stage | T1 narrative | T2 explanation | T3 persuasive | T4 poetry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **FILL** | Memories and senses | Facts, checked | Reasons, evidence, and the other side's | **Things I actually looked at** |
| **FIND** | School community; the **arc** | A Year 3 buddy; the **sequence** | The person who can say yes; the **case** | An audience who will **hear** it; the **shape on the page** |
| **SHAPE** | "Is it vivid?" | "Is it clear?" | "Would it convince someone who disagrees?" | **"Does every word earn its place?"** |
| **SHARE** | "Did you feel it?" | "Did you understand it?" | "Were you convinced?" | **"What stayed with you?"** |

**The four SHAPE questions go on the wall together in Week 6** — vivid → clear → convincing → does every word earn its place. Four questions, one move: *stop being the writer and become the reader.* It is the tidiest summary of the year anywhere in the programme.

**Teaching and recall schedule**

| Week | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1** | **Cold recall** — the fourth and final one. Then: "a poem can be nine words and a fragment, and it still goes through all four stages." |
| **2** | **Transfer — FILL.** Three columns on the board (memories / facts / evidence); what goes in the fourth? |
| **3** | **Transfer — FIND.** The first genre where the *layout* is part of the meaning. |
| **4** | **Spaced recall** — quick-fire, no notes. |
| **5** | **Four genres, one process** — each stage explained four ways; the class regenerates the four constants. |
| **6** | **Spaced recall + peg**, and the four SHAPE questions go up together. |
| **7** | **Transfer — SHAPE.** In three terms, improving meant adding. Here it means taking away — and the words are yours. |
| **8** | **Transfer — SHARE.** Two readings, five seconds of silence, and nobody has to explain their poem. |
| **9** | **The whole year** — the cycle drawn over four terms of the students' own work, physically laid out. |
| **10** | **Assess + hand it on** — explain the process across four genres, then write it for next year's Year 5s. |

**The four constants** (generated in Term 3, regenerated in Week 5, assessed in Week 10): you always fill before you draft · you always write to a particular someone · you always read it as the person who doesn't already agree · you always test it on a real human before you publish.

> **Week 1, Wednesday — teach the spotlight FIRST.** The cold recall must happen before anything reminds the class of the answer, including the routine itself. That lesson's card in the app carries a "teach the spotlight first today" banner. Normal order resumes the next day.

## PART E — WEEK BY WEEK

*Full daily lesson plans are in the app. This is the overview.*

### Week 1 — Looking Properly *(FILL)*
- **Mon — What a poem actually does.** Two poems on one subject: one written from a desk, one written from looking. Build the "What a Poem Does" chart; launch the noticing jotter.
- **Tue — Two minutes of looking.** The teacher looks at a leaf out loud for two minutes; the good noticings arrive after sixty seconds. Students do the same.
- **Wed — Show me what you saw.** Statement → image ("the classroom was messy" → "someone's jersey has been under the same chair since Tuesday"). *(Spotlight: cold recall.)*
- **Thu — Noticings into lines.** Cross out until it's a poem — thirty-two words become fourteen.
- **Fri — Two readings and a silence.** The listening protocol taught and practised.

### Week 2 — Fresh Images *(FILL)*
- **Mon — Borrowed vs true.** Five comparisons fast, then choose; the cliché graveyard goes on the wall. *(Spotlight: transfer — FILL.)*
- **Tue — Making the comparison do a job.** "Soft old leather gloves" vs "two maps".
- **Wed — Nouns and verbs do the work.** Delete every adjective, then strengthen one verb.
- **Thu — The image bank.** Collect ten; you will use five. Choosing is what makes it good.
- **Fri — The image gallery.** Two tests: can I see it, and is there a question living inside it?

### Week 3 — The Shape on the Page *(FILL → FIND)*
- **Mon — Where the line breaks.** Nine words, three shapes, three different poems. *(Spotlight: transfer — FIND.)*
- **Tue — Stanzas and silence.** Move the gap and watch the meaning move.
- **Wed — Choosing a form.** Free verse · list poem · small-moment · shape poem.
- **Thu — The audience card, poetry version.** They will *hear* this once. Draft freely.
- **Fri — The line-break test.** A partner marks where your voice pauses; compare with your breaks.

### Week 4 — Poem One: A Small Thing *(FIND)*
- **Mon — Titles and first lines.** A naming title, a framing title, and one that isn't in the poem at all.
- **Tue — Endings that land.** Delete your last line; nine times out of ten it's better.
- **Wed — Poem one, finished.** The three questions: can a stranger see it · does every word earn its place · does it stop at the right moment. *(Spotlight: spaced recall.)*
- **Thu — Poem two.** Deliberately different in subject, form or mood.
- **Fri — Poem one aloud.** Slow, pause at the breaks, three seconds of silence, no apologising.

### Week 5 — Sound *(FIND)*
- **Mon — Long line, short line.** The short line only works because the ones before it were long.
- **Tue — Repetition that builds.** Threes: ordinary → particular → the one that means something.
- **Wed — Sound without showing off.** One sound effect that earns its place, not six.
- **Thu — Finish poem two, start poem three.**
- **Fri — Four genres, one process.** *(Spotlight.)* Then poem two aloud.

### Week 6 — Poem Three: Someone, or a Year *(FIND)*
- **Mon — People are their habits.** Never write "kind" — write the orange peeled in one piece.
- **Tue — The turn.** The last two lines that make you reread the whole poem.
- **Wed — The set of three.** Range check: different subject, mood and shape. Shortest first, strongest last. *(Spotlight: recall + peg; the four SHAPE questions.)*
- **Thu — Finish the set.** Long write; every poem gets a title.
- **Fri — Build the criteria.** The class writes "What Makes a Strong Poem". Best-line share.

### Week 7 — Cutting *(SHAPE)*
- **Mon — Strong poem, weak poem.** Diagnose the weak exemplar, then find the real poem hiding inside it. *(Spotlight: transfer — SHAPE.)*
- **Tue — Marking up against the criteria.** The teacher finds their own weakest line first.
- **Wed — The cutting pass.** Delete about a third: really, very, just, so, little, old, beautiful, amazing.
- **Thu — One goal per poem.** Three small changes beat twelve intentions.
- **Fri — Preparing for the Helping Circle.** Fishbowl on the teacher; nobody has to explain their poem. *(Spotlight: handover.)*

### Week 8 — The Helping Circle & Reading Aloud *(SHARE)*
- **Mon — Circle day 1.** Two readings, five-second silence, two stars, one wish. *(Spotlight: transfer — SHARE.)*
- **Tue — Circle day 2 + triage.** Fix the picture; don't fix the meaning.
- **Wed — Revision workshops.** ADD · CUT · REORDER · REPLACE — with CUT dominant.
- **Thu — Reading a poem so it lands.** Slow, pause, quiet, and three seconds of silence at the end.
- **Fri — The poem swap.** Someone else reads your poem aloud; every stumble marks a line to look at.

### Week 9 — Best Copies and the Year Behind Us *(SHARE)*
- **Mon — The final edit.** Which rules are errors and which are choices — and being able to say why.
- **Tue — The anthology page.** One poem per page; white space is the frame.
- **Wed — Four terms, one writer.** The portfolio spread: all four published pieces on the desk at once. *(Spotlight: the whole year.)*
- **Thu — The year-in-review reflection.** Five questions; name a piece, quote a line, admit one hard thing.
- **Fri — Rehearsal.** Choose the poem that works best *in the air*, not on the page.

### Week 10 — Publish, Perform, Hand It On *(SHARE)*
- **Mon — Typing for the anthology.** Where poems get broken: autocapitalise, wrapping lines, lost stanza gaps.
- **Tue — Making the anthology.** Cover, contents with every name, dedication, editor's job.
- **Wed — Dress rehearsal.** Walk up, wait for silence, announce, read, hold, walk back.
- **Thu — The celebration.** Every student reads one poem; anthologies handed out.
- **Fri — Hand it on.** Explain the process across four genres, then write it for next year's Year 5s. *(Spotlight: assessment.)*

### The Helping Circle — Term 4 (poetry) version

Groups of four · five minutes per poet. **Two readings, then five seconds of silence.**

*Stars:* "The image I can still see is…" · "The line I'd remember tomorrow is…" · "I liked the sound of…" · "The ending made me…"
*Wishes:* "I lost the picture at…" · "I think ____ could go." · "That comparison felt like one I'd heard before." · "Could the line break somewhere else?" · "The last line explained it to me — could you trust me instead?"

**The rule that's unique to this term:** nobody has to explain their poem, and nobody may ask them to. Fix the *picture*; don't fix the *meaning*.

### Exemplars
Three short WAGOLL poems (an object, a wonder, a person) and a deliberately weaker rhyming "Nature" poem are in the app's Teacher tab with annotation notes. The weak one is deliberately the kind of poem school often teaches — the Week 7 discussion isn't "what's wrong with it" but **"what would we cut to find the real poem inside it?"**

### Differentiation
Full table in the app. The three that matter most this term:
- **"I can't think of anything"** → don't ask for an idea, hand them an object.
- **Only writes rhyming poems** → don't ban it, test it: "cross out every word you chose only because it rhymed." Whatever survives is the poem.
- **Writes something raw or private** → nobody has to share anything, and anything can appear in the anthology as "Anonymous". Make the offer publicly so nobody has to ask.

## PART G — THE YEAR, FINISHED

Four terms, four genres, one process. By the last Friday students should be able to say the sentence the whole year was built toward:

> *"I filled before I drafted. I wrote to one particular person. I read it as someone who didn't already agree. And I tested it on a real human being before I published it."*

The final task is a handover: each student writes the process down, in their own words with their own examples, for next year's Year 5s. Those pages go on the wall for the incoming class, and the four wall cards are handed to the students who can explain them best.

**For next year:** the reflections and handover letters are the most useful thing you can pass to the next teacher. They show exactly which stage each student owns and which one they still find hard — better than any mark.
